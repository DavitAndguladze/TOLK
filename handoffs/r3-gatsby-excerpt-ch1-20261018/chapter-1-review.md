# Gatsby Chapter 1 excerpt — review only

No fidelity or human approval. No ready artifact.

In my younger and more vulnerable years my father gave me some advice
that I’ve been turning over in my mind ever since.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just
remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages
that you’ve had.”

He didn’t say any more, but we’ve always been unusually communicative
in a reserved way, and I understood that he meant a great deal more
than that. In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a
habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me
the victim of not a few veteran bores. The abnormal mind is quick to
detect and attach itself to this quality when it appears in a normal
person, and so it came about that in college I was unjustly accused of
being a politician, because I was privy to the secret griefs of wild,
unknown men. Most of the confidences were unsought—frequently I have
feigned sleep, preoccupation, or a hostile levity when I realized by
some unmistakable sign that an intimate revelation was quivering on
the horizon; for the intimate revelations of young men, or at least
the terms in which they express them, are usually plagiaristic and
marred by obvious suppressions. Reserving judgements is a matter of
infinite hope. I am still a little afraid of missing something if I
forget that, as my father snobbishly suggested, and I snobbishly
repeat, a sense of the fundamental decencies is parcelled out
unequally at birth.

And, after boasting this way of my tolerance, I come to the admission
that it has a limit. Conduct may be founded on the hard rock or the
wet marshes, but after a certain point I don’t care what it’s founded
on. When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted
the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever; I
wanted no more riotous excursions with privileged glimpses into the
human heart. Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was
exempt from my reaction—Gatsby, who represented everything for which I
have an unaffected scorn. If personality is an unbroken series of
successful gestures, then there was something gorgeous about him, some
heightened sensitivity to the promises of life, as if he were related
to one of those intricate machines that register earthquakes ten
thousand miles away. This responsiveness had nothing to do with that
flabby impressionability which is dignified under the name of the
“creative temperament”—it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a
romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and
which it is not likely I shall ever find again. No—Gatsby turned out
all right at the end; it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust
floated in the wake of his dreams that temporarily closed out my
interest in the abortive sorrows and short-winded elations of men.

My family have been prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle
Western city for three generations. The Carraways are something of a
clan, and we have a tradition that we’re descended from the Dukes of
Buccleuch, but the actual founder of my line was my grandfather’s
brother, who came here in fifty-one, sent a substitute to the Civil
War, and started the wholesale hardware business that my father
carries on today.

I never saw this great-uncle, but I’m supposed to look like him—with
special reference to the rather hard-boiled painting that hangs in
father’s office. I graduated from New Haven in 1915, just a quarter of
a century after my father, and a little later I participated in that
delayed Teutonic migration known as the Great War. I enjoyed the
counter-raid so thoroughly that I came back restless. Instead of being
the warm centre of the world, the Middle West now seemed like the
ragged edge of the universe—so I decided to go East and learn the bond
business. Everybody I knew was in the bond business, so I supposed it
could support one more single man. All my aunts and uncles talked it
over as if they were choosing a prep school for me, and finally said,
“Why—ye-es,” with very grave, hesitant faces. Father agreed to finance
me for a year, and after various delays I came East, permanently, I
thought, in the spring of twenty-two.

The practical thing was to find rooms in the city, but it was a warm
season, and I had just left a country of wide lawns and friendly
trees, so when a young man at the office suggested that we take a
house together in a commuting town, it sounded like a great idea. He
found the house, a weather-beaten cardboard bungalow at eighty a
month, but at the last minute the firm ordered him to Washington, and
I went out to the country alone. I had a dog—at least I had him for a
few days until he ran away—and an old Dodge and a Finnish woman, who
made my bed and cooked breakfast and muttered Finnish wisdom to
herself over the electric stove.

## Proposed L1

When I was young, it was easy to hurt me. My father told me what he thought I should do. I have thought about his words ever since. He said, "Think before saying bad things about people. Not everyone has had the good things you have had." He said no more. We could tell each other much with few words. I knew he meant much more than that. So I often wait before I judge anyone. This can help me learn about many strange people. Some talk a long time without saying anything interesting. An abnormal mind can quickly see this in someone normal. Such people want to share things with that person. People at college called me a politician. That was not fair. I knew the secret pain of wild, unknown men. I did not ask for most of their secret talk. I would act as if I had gone to sleep. Sometimes I would act busy, or make fun of them. I did this when I knew secret talk was coming. Young men often use words they have taken from others. You can also see that they leave things out. To wait before we judge takes hope without an end. I am still afraid I might miss something. I must remember what my father said. Some people are born with more good in them. He said this as if we were better than others. I say it in that way too. I have been boasting about my tolerance. But there are things I cannot put up with. The way people act may rest on hard rock. Or it may rest on wet marshes. After a point, I do not care which it is. Last fall, I came back from the East. I wanted the world in uniform. It should stand at moral attention forever. I wanted no more riotous excursions. They gave me privileged glimpses into the human heart. Only Gatsby did not make me feel that way. This book has his name. Gatsby was everything I really looked down on. What if personality is an unbroken series of successful gestures? Then there was something gorgeous about him. He had great sensitivity to the promises of life. He was like machines that register earthquakes 10,000 miles away. This was not that flabby impressionability. I mean that things change you too much. People make that sound important: “creative temperament.” His was an extraordinary gift for hope. He had a romantic readiness for what life might bring. I have never found this in anyone else. I probably never will. No, Gatsby turned out all right in the end. But something preyed on Gatsby. Foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams. That made me lose interest in the sorrows of men. Their sorrows came to nothing. Their happy times did not last. I lost interest in those happy times too. This was only for a while. My family has been well-to-do and important here. This is a city in the Middle West. This has been true for three generations. The Carraways are a close family. We say our family comes from the Dukes of Buccleuch. But my grandfather’s brother really started our family line. He came here in '51. He sent another man to the Civil War. That man went in his place. He started a business. He would sell things used to make and fix things. He would sell them to other business people. My father is still in charge of that business. I never saw this great-uncle. People say I look like him. The painting hangs in the office of my father. He looks like a hard man in that painting. I finished school at New Haven in 1915. My father finished there 25 years before me. A little later, I took part in the Great War. I call it a late “Teutonic migration.” I mean a war, as if people were just moving. I did enjoy fighting back so much. I came home restless because of that. I once felt the Middle West was a warm centre. It felt like the centre of the world. Now it felt like the ragged edge of the universe. So I decided to go East. I would learn the bond business. Everyone I knew worked in that business. I thought it could pay one more single man. All my aunts and uncles talked about my plan. They talked as if choosing a prep school for me. At last, they said, "Well, yes." They looked very serious and not quite sure. My father agreed to give me money for a year. After some delays, I came East in spring, 1922. I thought I would stay there for good. To find rooms in the city made the most sense. But the days were warm. I had just left wide lawns and friendly trees. A young man at work had an idea. We could share a house in a town. We would go into the city for work each day. That sounded like a great idea. He found a weather-beaten bungalow. It was like a house made of cardboard. It was 80 a month. At the last moment, work sent him to Washington. So I went to the house in the country alone. I had a dog for a few days. Then he ran away. I also had an old Dodge and a Finnish woman. She made my bed and cooked breakfast. She muttered Finnish wisdom to herself. She did this over the electric stove.
